Overview

In this homework I built a software rasterizer that turns vector (SVG) descriptions of 2D geometry into pixels. I started with the most basic primitive, filling a single-color triangle by testing point-in-triangle coverage inside a bounding box, and then layered on the pieces a real rendering pipeline needs: supersampling for antialiasing, hierarchical transforms for composing shapes, barycentric coordinates for interpolating per-vertex attributes, and finally texture mapping with both pixel-level filtering (nearest and bilinear) and level-of-detail filtering using mipmaps (trilinear).

The thread running through the whole assignment is sampling and antialiasing. Rendering is really the problem of reconstructing a continuous signal (the ideal image) onto a discrete grid of pixels, and pretty much everything from supersampling to mipmapping is just a different way of cutting down the aliasing you get when you sample a signal below its Nyquist rate. The thing I found most interesting was how the same core idea, averaging more information per pixel, keeps coming back in different forms. Sometimes I do that averaging in screen space (supersampling) and sometimes in texture space (mipmaps), and each version trades off speed, memory, and antialiasing quality in its own way.

Task 1: Drawing Single-Color Triangles

How I rasterize a triangle. Given the three screen-space vertices, I first compute the signed area of the triangle, \( \text{area} = (x_2-x_0)(y_1-y_0) - (y_2-y_0)(x_1-x_0) \). If the area is zero the triangle is degenerate and I just return. If it is negative the vertices are in clockwise order, so I swap two of them to keep the winding consistent. That way I can use a single sign convention for the inside test no matter how the triangle was specified.

Next I compute the triangle's bounding box, meaning the smallest axis-aligned rectangle that still contains all three vertices. I get it by taking the min and max of the x and y coordinates, flooring and ceiling to pixel boundaries, and clamping to the screen (\([0, \text{width}-1] \times [0, \text{height}-1]\)) so I never test off-screen pixels. Then I build three Edge objects, one per triangle side. Each edge stores a point and a direction and evaluates the implicit line (half-plane) function

\[ E(p) = \text{sign}\cdot\big((p_x - a_x)\, d_y - (p_y - a_y)\, d_x\big) \]

whose sign tells me which side of the edge a point is on. I loop over every pixel in the bounding box, evaluate the three edge functions at the pixel center \((x+0.5, y+0.5)\), and fill the pixel if it lands on the inside (non-negative) side of all three edges at once. To keep ties consistent I canonicalize each edge in make_edge so that two triangles sharing an edge compute the exact same edge value at every sample. That keeps shared edges free of gaps. I did not implement the top-left rule, and my test counts samples on an edge as inside, so a sample that lands exactly on a shared edge gets drawn by both triangles. The spec allows this.

Why this is no worse than the naive bounding-box approach. The naive version checks every sample inside the bounding box and, for each one, tests whether it is inside the triangle. My algorithm is really the same bounding-box approach: I only ever visit samples inside that same clamped box, and for each sample I do a fixed amount of work, just three edge-function evaluations that are each a couple of multiplies and adds. I never touch samples outside the box, and I precompute the edge coefficients once per triangle in make_edge instead of recomputing them at every sample. So the total cost is \(O(\text{bounding-box area})\) edge tests, which is the same asymptotic cost as the naive scan and never more work than it.

Task 2: Antialiasing by Supersampling

Algorithm and data structures. Supersampling cuts down aliasing by taking more than one sample per pixel and averaging them. The main data structure I use is a sample_buffer of size \(\text{width} \times \text{height} \times \text{sample rate}\). Instead of one color per pixel, I store sample_rate colors per pixel. For a sample rate \(N\) I treat the pixel as an \(\sqrt{N} \times \sqrt{N}\) grid (sqrt_rate = round(sqrt(sample_rate))) and put one sample at the center of each sub-cell, at offsets \(\big(\frac{s_x + 0.5}{\sqrt N}, \frac{s_y + 0.5}{\sqrt N}\big)\) inside the pixel.

Changes I made to the pipeline. There were three:

  • set_sample_rate reallocates the sample buffer to the new \(\text{width}\cdot\text{height}\cdot\text{sample rate}\) size whenever the rate changes.
  • The triangle rasterizer now loops over the \(\sqrt N \times \sqrt N\) sub-samples of each pixel in the bounding box, runs the same three-edge inside test at each sub-sample position, and writes the color into the matching slot of the sample buffer instead of straight into the framebuffer. Points and lines just fill all sub-samples of a pixel with the same color.
  • resolve_to_framebuffer is the new downsampling step. After everything is drawn, it averages the \(N\) sub-sample colors of each pixel (a box filter) and writes that averaged, clamped color into the final RGB framebuffer.
  • set_framebuffer_target also reallocates the sample buffer to match the new size when the window is resized, and clear_buffers resets every entry of the sample buffer to the background color at the start of each frame so stale samples never leak across redraws.

Why supersampling antialiases. With a single sample per pixel the rasterizer makes a hard yes-or-no call about whether the whole pixel is inside the triangle, and that is what gives you the stair-stepped, jaggy edges. When I take many sub-samples and average them, a pixel that is only partly covered ends up as a blend of the triangle color and the background, weighted by how much of it is actually covered. That approximates the real area coverage of the pixel, so edges turn into smooth gradients instead of hard steps.

In signal-processing terms, averaging \(N\) sub-samples is a box filter over the pixel area applied before the final downsample. It raises the effective sampling rate by a factor of \(\sqrt N\) in each direction, which pushes the aliasing up to higher frequencies rather than removing it outright. That is why a thin enough sliver can still break up even at 16 samples per pixel: it just takes a finer feature to alias than it did at rate 1.

Why these results look the way they do. All three images show basic/test4.svg at default parameters with the pixel inspector over a very skinny triangle corner. At sample rate 1 the thin corner falls apart into isolated fully-on or fully-off pixels. Where the triangle is narrower than a pixel it either grabs the whole pixel or misses it, so the tip looks broken and aliased. As I bump the rate up to 4 and then 16, the partly-covered pixels along the corner take on in-between shades based on how much of the pixel the sliver really covers, and the corner smooths out into a clean taper. More sub-samples just means a finer estimate of coverage and smoother antialiasing, and I pay for it with more computation and memory.

Task 3: Transforms

SVG groups (<g>) apply transforms hierarchically, so each group's transform is composed with its parent's and children pick up everything above them in the tree. One detail that matters here: rotate(a) always turns around the origin of the current coordinate system, not around some arbitrary joint. So to pivot the arm at the shoulder I lean on the parent group's translate. The right arm lives inside <g transform="translate(90 -40)">, which moves the local origin out to the shoulder, and only then do I apply rotate(-75). Because that rotation runs in the translated frame, it turns the arm around the shoulder rather than around the body's center. For my_robot.svg I used this to make cubeman wave hello: the rotate(-75) swings the upper arm up and out, and then a nested <g transform="translate(70 0)"><g transform="rotate(30)"> moves the origin down the arm to the elbow and rotates the forearm about that point, cocking the hand to the side. To add a bit of life I also gave the right leg a small rotate(15) like he is mid-step, recolored the torso and legs blue, and turned the head yellow while leaving the arms red for contrast. Everything is done by nesting <g transform="..."> elements (translate, rotate, scale) around the existing polygons, so rotating a parent group rotates the whole limb as one rigid piece.

Task 4: Barycentric coordinates

Barycentric coordinates let me write any point inside a triangle as a weighted average of the triangle's three vertices. For vertices \(V_0, V_1, V_2\) and weights \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\) with \(\alpha + \beta + \gamma = 1\), the point is \(P = \alpha V_0 + \beta V_1 + \gamma V_2\). The intuition I like is that each weight measures how much of the corresponding vertex a point is: \(\alpha = 1\) at \(V_0\) and drops linearly to \(0\) along the opposite edge, and the same holds for \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\). You can also read each weight as the area of the sub-triangle opposite that vertex divided by the total area. Because they sum to one and change linearly across the triangle, they are the natural tool for interpolating any per-vertex attribute, which here is color.

I compute them with the standard formula using a shared denominator \( d = (y_1-y_2)(x_0-x_2) + (x_2-x_1)(y_0-y_2) \):

\[ \alpha = \frac{(y_1-y_2)(p_x-x_2) + (x_2-x_1)(p_y-y_2)}{d}, \quad \beta = \frac{(y_2-y_0)(p_x-x_2) + (x_0-x_2)(p_y-y_2)}{d}, \quad \gamma = 1 - \alpha - \beta \]

At each covered sample I then blend the vertex colors as \( C = \alpha C_0 + \beta C_1 + \gamma C_2 \). When the three vertices are pure red, green, and blue, this gives a smoothly blended color triangle where the color near each vertex is dominated by that vertex's hue and transitions continuously through the middle.

Task 5: "Pixel sampling" for texture mapping

Pixel sampling is the step that decides, for each screen sample covered by a textured triangle, which texture color (texel) to use. I implemented it in rasterize_textured_triangle. For every covered sub-sample I use the same barycentric coordinates from Task 4, except this time I interpolate the per-vertex texture coordinates \((u, v)\) rather than colors. I pack that \((u,v)\) into a SampleParams struct, along with the \((u,v)\) of the neighboring screen pixels that I need later for level sampling, and hand it to the texture's sampling routine to get a color back.

Nearest vs. bilinear. These are two ways to turn a continuous \((u,v)\) into a color:

  • Nearest (sample_nearest): I scale \((u,v)\) into texel space by multiplying by \((\text{width}-1)\) and \((\text{height}-1)\), add \(0.5\) and floor so the coordinate snaps to the closest texel, clamp the result to the valid texel range so border lookups stay in bounds, and return that texel's color. It is fast but blocky, since neighboring screen pixels can snap to the same texel or jump abruptly to the next one.
  • Bilinear (sample_bilinear): I scale \((u,v)\) into texel space the same way, take the lower-left texel \((x_0, y_0)\) as the floor of that coordinate, and use the fractional parts \(s, t\) as blend weights. I fetch the four surrounding texels \((x_0, y_0)\), \((x_0+1, y_0)\), \((x_0, y_0+1)\), \((x_0+1, y_0+1)\), each clamped to the border, then do two horizontal lerps between the left and right texels and one vertical lerp between those results. That gives a smooth blend instead of a hard jump.

Relative differences. Bilinear comes out visibly smoother than nearest, and the gap is clearest at 1 sample per pixel, where nearest shows hard blocky squares along high-contrast texture features and bilinear blends them away. The two settings are actually fixing different problems, and it is worth being precise about which is which. Under magnification, where one texel covers many screen pixels, nearest acts like a box reconstruction filter over the texels, so each texel shows up as a hard square. That is a reconstruction problem, not aliasing, and bilinear fixes it with a smoother tent filter. Supersampling does little here, because all the sub-samples inside a pixel tend to land in the same texel. Under minification, where one pixel covers many texels, supersampling does help, because it averages several texel lookups per pixel, which is real antialiasing. So the difference between nearest and bilinear is biggest under magnification or on high-frequency detail, and when the texture sits at roughly 1:1 scale the two look almost the same.

Task 6: "Level Sampling" with mipmaps for texture mapping

Level sampling picks which mipmap level to read from, and it is aimed at texture minification aliasing, the case where a single screen pixel covers many texels. A mipmap is a pyramid of pre-filtered, successively half-sized copies of the texture, so reading from a coarser level is basically averaging a lot of texels at once, which is exactly what an undersampled pixel wants. To choose the level I estimate how fast the texture coordinates change per screen pixel. In get_level I take the \((u,v)\) differences to the neighboring screen pixels in x and y, scale them by the texture dimensions to get the footprint in texels, and compute

\[ L = \log_2\Big(\max\big(\big\| \tfrac{\partial (u,v)}{\partial x}\big\|,\ \big\| \tfrac{\partial (u,v)}{\partial y}\big\|\big)\Big) \]

From there I support three level-sampling modes. L_ZERO always samples the full-resolution level 0, L_NEAREST rounds \(L\) to the nearest integer level, and L_LINEAR samples the two levels that bracket \(L\) and blends them by the fractional part of \(L\), which gives trilinear filtering when I pair it with bilinear pixel sampling. Before using \(L\) I clamp it to the range from 0 to the last mip level, so requests never fall off either end of the pyramid. Each level is itself sampled with whichever pixel method (nearest or bilinear) is selected. One limitation of this approach: taking the max of the two derivatives collapses the footprint down to a single number, so when the footprint is stretched much more in one direction than the other the chosen level is too coarse along the short axis and the texture over-blurs. That directional case is exactly what anisotropic filtering is meant to fix.

Tradeoffs (speed, memory, antialiasing).

  • More samples per pixel (supersampling): the strongest and most general antialiasing, since it fixes both edges and textures, but also the most expensive. Both the cost and the sample-buffer memory grow linearly with the sample rate.
  • Pixel sampling (nearest to bilinear): bilinear costs roughly 4× the texel fetches of nearest and adds smoothing under magnification, but it does nothing for minification aliasing. The memory overhead is basically nothing.
  • Level sampling (mipmaps): very cheap antialiasing for minification, since it is roughly constant work per sample no matter how much the texture shrinks, and it costs about 33% extra texture memory to store the pyramid. L_LINEAR is about twice the cost of L_NEAREST because it does two level lookups, but it hides the visible seams where the level would otherwise pop from one to the next.

So the short version is: supersampling buys antialiasing power with time and memory, mipmaps buy cheap minification antialiasing for a fixed memory overhead, and bilinear buys magnification smoothness for a small, constant cost.

I used mine.png, a photo of the Campanile at sunset, which I wired in by copying one of the svg/texmap/ files and pointing its texture at my image. I picked it because it is full of thin, high-frequency detail (the city lights, the trees, and the fine vertical lines of the tower) that aliases badly once it is shrunk down. This rasterizer is 2D with affine texture mapping, so there is no perspective and nothing is literally "far away." The minification here comes from the texture being large (about 1024 pixels wide) mapped into a much smaller region on screen, and all four screenshots are zoomed out to that same minified scale so the mip levels actually come into play. Walking through the four:

  • L_ZERO, P_NEAREST: always reads the full-resolution texture and snaps to one texel, so the minified detail sparkles and speckles. The lights and thin lines flicker into harsh, noisy dots because a single texel is standing in for a whole cluster of them.
  • L_ZERO, P_LINEAR: still the full-resolution level, so it is only a little better. Bilinear softens the individual texels but does nothing about minification, so the same aliasing is mostly still there, just slightly smoother.
  • L_NEAREST, P_NEAREST: now the level selector jumps to a coarser, pre-averaged mip level, and the sparkle largely disappears. The detail reads as a clean, averaged version of itself, though because it is nearest-in-level you can occasionally catch a small step where the level switches.
  • L_NEAREST, P_LINEAR: the smoothest of the four. It pulls from the coarser mip level and then blends within it, so the minified texture looks clean with neither the sparkle of L_ZERO nor the blockiness of nearest pixel sampling.
